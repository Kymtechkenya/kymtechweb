Buying Guide

How to Choose the Best Laptop for College or University in Kenya

Published 8 September 2026 · 7 min read

Every year we get the same message on WhatsApp around late August and early January: "I'm joining campus next month, which laptop should I get?" It's a fair question, and a slightly stressful one — you're usually buying one laptop that has to survive three or four years of assignments, group projects, and at least one all-nighter before a deadline. Here's how to think about it properly, instead of just buying whatever's cheapest in the shop window.

Start with the specs that actually matter

Laptop spec sheets throw a lot of numbers at you, but for a student, only a handful of them change how the laptop actually feels to use day to day.

Processor (CPU)

This is the laptop's "brain speed". For general coursework — Word, browser tabs, PowerPoint, Zoom calls — an Intel Core i5 or AMD Ryzen 5 handles everything comfortably. Avoid entry-level Celeron or Pentium chips if you can; they're fine for very light use, but they slow down fast once you've got ten browser tabs and a Word document open at the same time, which is basically every study session.

RAM (memory)

8GB is the realistic minimum in 2026 — anything less and you'll feel it the moment you have a browser, a PDF reader, and Word open together. If your course involves heavier software (we'll get to which ones below), 16GB is worth paying extra for; it's one of the cheapest upgrades that makes the biggest difference to how "stuck" the laptop feels under pressure.

Storage

Get an SSD, not a traditional hard drive (HDD) — this alone is the difference between a laptop that boots in 15 seconds and one that boots in two minutes. 256GB is enough for most students once you're using cloud storage (Google Drive, OneDrive) for backups; go to 512GB if you'll be storing a lot of design files, videos, or software installers locally.

Battery life & weight

Not every lecture hall has enough sockets for forty laptops, and you won't always want to carry a charger to a 6-hour day on campus. Look for a realistic 5+ hours of battery life, and — if you'll be walking or matatu-ing around campus daily — a laptop under about 1.8kg. A 15-inch gaming laptop looks great in the shop but gets old fast in a backpack.

What to prioritise, by course

This is where a lot of students overspend on power they don't need, or underspend and hit a wall halfway through the semester. Match the laptop to what you'll actually be running.

  • Business, humanities, social sciences, education: a light, long-battery-life laptop with a Core i5 and 8GB RAM is genuinely enough. Prioritise portability and battery over raw power here.
  • Computer Science / Software Engineering: go for 16GB RAM if your budget allows it — running an IDE, a browser with documentation open, and a local server or emulator at the same time eats memory fast. A Core i5/i7 or Ryzen 5/7 is a comfortable baseline.
  • Engineering, architecture (AutoCAD, Revit, SolidWorks): these programs lean on both RAM and graphics. 16GB RAM plus a dedicated (not just integrated) graphics card will save you real frustration compared to a general-purpose laptop.
  • Design, film, media (Photoshop, Premiere, Illustrator): similar to engineering — 16GB RAM minimum, and a screen with accurate colour matters more here than almost any other spec on this list.
  • Nursing, medicine, general sciences: mostly document- and browser-based work with the occasional lab software — a solid Core i5 with 8GB RAM covers this well, so you can put the savings toward books and equipment instead.

Ex-UK refurbished or brand new?

This is the single biggest lever on price. An Ex-UK business laptop — a ThinkPad, EliteBook, or Latitude that was previously used in a UK office — gets you a genuinely higher-spec machine (often a Core i5 or i7 with 8–16GB RAM) for roughly what a weak, budget "brand new" laptop costs. The trade-off is a laptop that's a few years old and has visible signs of previous use, graded accordingly.

For most students, a Grade A Ex-UK laptop with a warranty is the better deal: same day-to-day performance, lower price, money left over for the rest of your term. Brand new starts to make more sense once your budget comfortably clears about KSh 65,000–70,000, where new machines start matching or beating Ex-UK specs at a similar price point.

Realistic budgets in KSh

These move a little with the market, but as a rough guide for late 2026:

  • Under KSh 30,000: entry Ex-UK laptops — fine for typing, browsing, and light coursework, but keep expectations modest on speed.
  • KSh 30,000–50,000: the sweet spot for most students — Ex-UK Core i5/i7 business laptops with 8–16GB RAM.
  • KSh 50,000–90,000: higher-spec Ex-UK workstation-class laptops (good for engineering/design coursework), or a new mid-range laptop.
  • KSh 90,000+: new laptops with dedicated graphics — usually only necessary for gaming-adjacent or heavy rendering coursework.

A quick checklist before you buy

  • Ask what grade the Ex-UK unit is (A, A-, B) and what that grading actually inspects.
  • Confirm the warranty length in writing, not just verbally.
  • Check it has the ports you'll actually use — HDMI for presentations, enough USB ports, and ideally USB-C.
  • Test the keyboard and trackpad in person if you're buying from a physical store.
  • Ask about battery health specifically — it degrades with age and matters more than almost any other used-laptop spec.
